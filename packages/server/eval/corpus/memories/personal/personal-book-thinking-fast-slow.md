Reading notes from "Thinking, Fast and Slow" by Daniel Kahneman. Started reading March 10, about 70% through as of early April.

## Core Framework

Kahneman divides cognition into two systems:
- **System 1:** Fast, automatic, intuitive, emotional. Runs with little effort. Handles pattern matching, quick judgments, and familiar situations. Example: reading a facial expression, doing 2+2.
- **System 2:** Slow, deliberate, analytical, effortful. Required for complex calculations, careful reasoning, and novel problems. Example: computing 17 x 24, parallel parking in a tight spot.

The key insight is that System 1 operates constantly and System 2 is lazy — it often accepts System 1's suggestions without scrutiny. Most cognitive biases stem from System 1 generating a quick answer that System 2 fails to override.

## Key Concepts and Takeaways

**Anchoring:** Initial exposure to a number biases subsequent estimates. In negotiations, whoever sets the first number anchors the entire discussion. Relevant for pricing decisions — the first price a customer sees becomes their reference point.

**Loss Aversion:** Losses are felt roughly 2x as strongly as equivalent gains. People will go to irrational lengths to avoid losses. This explains why free trials work — once people have something, the loss of it feels worse than the gain of getting it.

**WYSIATI (What You See Is All There Is):** System 1 builds the best story from available information and doesn't account for what it doesn't know. This is why first impressions are so sticky and why we jump to conclusions with insufficient data.

**Peak-End Rule:** People judge experiences by their peak intensity and how they end, not by the sum or average. A great ending can redeem a mediocre experience. Implications for product UX — the onboarding flow and the last interaction matter disproportionately.

**Planning Fallacy:** People systematically underestimate the time and cost of future tasks. The fix: use reference class forecasting — look at how long similar projects took historically instead of planning from the inside.
